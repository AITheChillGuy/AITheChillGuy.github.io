Stroke Lesion Segmentation on MRI

Do complex architectures always perform better? A controlled benchmark of three, with identical loaders, augmentation, and loss.

Role
Research Assistant · Qatar University
Stack
PyTorch · UNet · Attention-UNet · ViT · Medical Imaging
Year
2024
Venue
IEEE Access
Status
Published

The problem. Stroke lesions on MRI are small, irregular, and heavily outnumbered by healthy tissue. Severe class imbalance is the kind of setting where more elaborate architectures are supposed to earn their keep.

The approach. A controlled benchmark of several deep learning architectures — classic UNet, attention-augmented UNet, and a Transformer-based variant — for stroke lesion segmentation on MRI, trained under matched conditions so the architecture itself is what's being measured.

The well-tuned simple baseline was not reliably beaten by added architectural complexity.

What was hard. Holding the comparison fair. For "simple vs. complex" to be a meaningful question, the training pipeline has to be steady enough across architectures that the architecture itself is the variable on the table.

What shipped. A peer-reviewed comparison in IEEE Access (2024) showing that, on this task, added architectural complexity did not reliably beat the well-tuned simple baseline.

What this changes. Controlled benchmarking as a default. It raises the bar for any "novel architecture" claim that hasn't actually held the rest of the pipeline fixed.

Zafari-Ghadim, Soliman, Yousif, Ibrahim, Rashed, Mabrok. "Deep Models for Stroke Segmentation: Do Complex Architectures Always Perform Better?" IEEE Access, 2024.
